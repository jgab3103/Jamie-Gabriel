Open Learning

Quality educational resources should be accessible to everyone.

I believe that access to high-quality educational resources should not depend on someone's financial circumstances. Knowledge is something we develop collectively, and sharing it openly benefits individuals, educational institutions and society more broadly.

This philosophy has developed over many years working across education, academic research, music and technology. It is also strongly influenced by my background in software development and the open-source community, where collaboration and the free exchange of knowledge have produced some of the most important technological developments of recent decades.

I believe these principles have an equally important place in education.

The challenge of finding quality educational resources

There is an extraordinary amount of educational material available online, but finding high-quality, freely accessible resources can be surprisingly difficult.

Teachers routinely spend considerable time searching for appropriate materials, often navigating websites filled with advertising, subscription prompts, restricted content and other commercial distractions.

This is particularly concerning in school classrooms, where teachers may find themselves relying on advertising-supported platforms simply because suitable alternatives are unavailable or unaffordable.

I don't see this as a criticism of teachers, who already work under considerable time and resource constraints. Rather, it raises a broader question about how we develop, fund and distribute educational resources.

Should students and teachers have to navigate advertising and commercial barriers simply to access good educational content?

I don't believe they should.

The issue is not that commercial educational resources have no place. Many excellent resources are developed by commercial organisations, and the expertise involved in creating them deserves recognition and appropriate compensation.

However, education serves a broader public purpose, and I believe there should always be high-quality alternatives that are freely accessible, particularly for materials intended for use in schools.

A teacher should be able to open an educational activity in front of a classroom without worrying about inappropriate advertising, distracting commercial content or whether students will be asked to pay to continue learning.

Learning from successful models

There are already excellent examples of organisations demonstrating that educational information can be made widely accessible without relying on conventional advertising or individual paywalls.

Wikipedia is perhaps the most recognisable example. Built through remarkable international collaboration, it provides free access to an enormous body of knowledge, supported primarily through voluntary contributions.

Khan Academy offers another important model. Its extensive collection of educational materials demonstrates how carefully designed digital resources can support independent learning, classroom teaching and lifelong education. Many of its resources can be accessed freely without requiring users to purchase subscriptions or create accounts.

These organisations operate in different ways, but they share an important principle: making knowledge accessible is central to their purpose, rather than simply a means of attracting paying customers.

The open-source software movement provides another useful parallel. Some of the world's most sophisticated technological infrastructure has been developed through communities that share their work, invite collaboration and recognise the collective benefits of openness.

These models demonstrate that freely accessible resources need not be simplistic, poorly maintained or inferior to commercial alternatives.

Indeed, some of the most valuable resources available to educators and learners are the result of precisely this kind of commitment to public access.

The democratisation of knowledge

Throughout my career, I've been fortunate to learn from extraordinary teachers, musicians, researchers and colleagues. Much of what I know has come from people who were generous with their knowledge, experience and time.

Sharing what I've learned feels like a natural continuation of that generosity.

I also believe that education is fundamentally about curiosity. The opportunity to explore an idea, experiment with a concept or discover something new shouldn't be restricted by financial barriers.

The internet provides an unprecedented opportunity to make knowledge accessible to people across geographic, social and economic boundaries.

Yet the existence of digital technology does not automatically guarantee equitable access to education. Subscription costs, advertising, restrictive licensing and technological barriers can reproduce many of the inequalities that digital learning has the potential to overcome.

For this reason, I believe accessibility needs to be a deliberate principle in the design and distribution of educational resources, rather than an incidental feature.

There is also a broader public benefit to making educational knowledge freely available. When a resource helps a student develop a new skill, supports a teacher in the classroom or encourages someone to pursue an intellectual interest, the benefits extend beyond the individual user.

Education is not simply a private commodity. It is also a public good.

The philosophy behind these resources

The music learning resources on this website reflect these ideas on a much smaller scale.

My aim is to develop high-quality educational materials that are freely accessible to students, teachers, parents and anyone interested in learning about music.

Some are straightforward worksheets and teaching aids. Others are interactive applications designed to help learners explore musical concepts through experimentation, listening and practice.

The collection includes resources for complete beginners alongside more advanced materials exploring harmony, improvisation, composition, acoustics, music history and music technology.

I approach their development with the same care that I bring to my own teaching, research and creative work. The intention is not simply to make resources available, but to create materials that are genuinely useful, educationally sound and enjoyable to use.

I also want them to be accessible in a practical sense.

No advertising No paywalls No paid subscriptions

A teacher should be able to open an activity in front of a class without worrying about inappropriate advertising or distracting commercial content.

A student should be able to explore an idea out of curiosity, without encountering an artificial barrier to further learning.

A parent should be able to support their child's musical development without needing to purchase yet another subscription.

And an independent learner should be able to explore advanced musical ideas simply because they find them interesting.

High-quality resources should be developed for learning, rather than for advertising revenue or commercial engagement.

Supporting open education

Of course, creating and maintaining high-quality educational resources requires considerable time, effort and expertise.

Interactive applications also involve ongoing software development, technical maintenance and hosting costs. As the collection grows, these demands will inevitably increase.

My intention is to keep these resources freely accessible while providing an opportunity for people who find them valuable to contribute voluntarily towards their ongoing development.

This follows a model familiar from Wikipedia and many open-source projects: people who are willing and able to contribute help sustain resources that remain available to everyone.

Contributions are entirely optional and do not provide preferential access.

Whether you contribute financially or not, you are equally welcome to use the resources.

I also recognise that financial contributions are only one way of supporting a project like this. Sharing resources with other educators, providing feedback, suggesting improvements and contributing ideas are equally valuable forms of participation.

The objective is not to create a commercial educational platform, but to establish a sustainable approach to developing and sharing useful educational materials.

An evolving project: SAJA Music

The resources are currently hosted on my personal website, jamiegabriel.org, but they also form part of a much larger project that we are developing through SAJA Music.

Our longer-term vision for SAJA Music is an extensive digital humanities project bringing together music education, musicology, academic research, technology and creative practice.

We hope to develop a growing collection of freely accessible educational resources, interactive applications and digital tools that encourage people to explore music from a range of perspectives.

These will encompass the practical experience of playing, listening and creating music, alongside the theoretical, historical, mathematical and computational ideas that underpin it.

I am particularly interested in the possibilities that digital technologies offer for making complex musical ideas more accessible.

Interactive applications can allow learners to experiment directly with concepts that might otherwise remain abstract. Musical structures can be explored visually and aurally, historical developments can be examined through interactive representations, and theoretical ideas can be investigated through experimentation rather than explanation alone.

Digital humanities approaches also offer exciting opportunities to connect musical works, composers, historical contexts, analytical methods and research findings in ways that conventional textbooks and teaching materials cannot easily achieve.

Our ambition is to bring these approaches together in a coherent collection of resources that supports learning, exploration and discovery across different levels of musical experience.

While the project is still developing, the underlying philosophy will remain the same: high-quality educational and research resources should be as widely accessible as possible.

Feedback and collaboration

An important part of this philosophy is recognising that educational resources are never really finished.

They can always be improved through experience, research and the contributions of others.

I particularly welcome feedback from teachers, students, musicians and researchers who use these resources.

If something doesn't work as expected, an explanation could be clearer, or you have an idea for a new activity or improvement, I'd love to hear about it.

I'm especially interested in hearing from classroom teachers about how these materials work in practice, what their students find useful and where there are gaps that better resources might help address.

This kind of feedback is particularly valuable because educational resources ultimately need to work in real learning environments, with students who bring different abilities, experiences, interests and needs.

It is also very much in the spirit of the open-source communities that have influenced my work: sharing ideas, learning from others and improving things collectively.

Over time, I hope that SAJA Music will become more than a collection of resources developed by a small number of people. Ideally, it will provide opportunities for collaboration among educators, musicians, researchers and developers who share an interest in making musical knowledge more accessible.

Good educational resources should not only be freely available. They should also be open to improvement through the experience and knowledge of the people who use them.

Looking ahead

This collection will continue to evolve as we develop new teaching materials, interactive learning tools, musical experiments and digital humanities resources.

Some will be designed for children taking their first steps in music. Others will explore advanced topics in harmony, improvisation, composition, musicology, acoustics and computational approaches to musical analysis.

My hope is that these resources will support classroom teachers, private music educators, students, researchers and independent learners, while making a small contribution to the wider movement towards accessible, high-quality education.

Throughout my career, I've benefited enormously from the generosity of people who have shared their knowledge and encouraged my curiosity.

Making these resources freely available is one way of continuing that tradition.

Ultimately, I hope they encourage others to explore music, ask questions, experiment with ideas and discover something they might otherwise never have encountered.

And if they help someone develop a lifelong curiosity about music and learning, all the better.
