How Synthesis Works

Music Synthesis Primer · Part 2 · Building and Shaping Sounds

A synthesiser turns ideas about waves into sound. But how does pressing a key become a note with a particular pitch, colour, shape and movement?

In Part 1, we explored the mathematics of sound. Now we'll assemble the building blocks of a synthesiser. We'll use a classic subtractive synthesiser as our starting model, while remembering that not every synthesis method uses the same architecture.

1. The signal path

Imagine a synthesiser as a chain of operations. Something generates an audio signal; other components change it; then the result reaches your speakers.

Keyboard / MIDI→Oscillator→Filter→Amplifier→Output

There is also a second kind of information: control signals. Envelopes and low-frequency oscillators (LFOs) can tell a filter to open or an amplifier to change its level. These signals usually control sound rather than being heard directly.

Two important ideas: Audio is the signal we hear. Modulation is a changing instruction that alters some property of the sound.

2. Oscillators: where sound begins

An oscillator generates a repeating signal. Its frequency determines the fundamental pitch; its waveform helps determine the harmonic content and therefore the timbre.

A sine wave has only one frequency component. Ideal sawtooth and square waves contain many harmonics, which makes them useful starting points for richer sounds. A triangle wave contains odd harmonics that diminish relatively quickly.

Many synthesisers have two or more oscillators. Slightly detuning them creates beating and a sense of thickness; tuning them to different intervals can produce chords or new spectral relationships.

3. Filters: shaping the spectrum

A filter changes the balance of frequencies already present in a sound. A low-pass filter reduces higher frequencies; a high-pass filter reduces lower ones; a band-pass filter emphasises a region between two cutoffs.

Cutoff frequency sets where filtering becomes significant. Resonance can emphasise frequencies near the cutoff, sometimes making a sound whistle or squelch.

Notice that filtering a sine wave cannot reveal harmonics that were never there. This is why waveform choice and filtering work together in subtractive synthesis.

4. Envelopes: shaping sound over time

Press a piano key and its sound begins sharply, then gradually fades. Bow a violin and the sound may swell slowly and continue while you bow. An envelope describes how a parameter changes over time.

The classic ADSR envelope has four stages: Attack (rise), Decay (fall to a sustained level), Sustain (level while the key is held) and Release (fall after key release).

Shape an ADSR envelope

The key is held for one second after decay, then released. This is a simplified linear envelope.

An envelope can control more than volume. Apply it to filter cutoff and a sound can begin bright, then become darker, even while the note remains held.

5. Modulation: making sounds move

A low-frequency oscillator (LFO) is an oscillator usually used to control another parameter rather than generate an audible note. Route an LFO to pitch for vibrato, to amplitude for tremolo, or to filter cutoff for a moving tone.

Parameter(t) = centre + depth × sin(2π × rate × t)Rate controls how fast the movement repeats; depth controls how far it moves.

Watch an LFO move a parameter

The modulation signal moves around a central parameter value.

Modulation can also come from keyboard velocity, an expression pedal, aftertouch, step sequencers, envelopes or other oscillators. The key idea is source → destination → amount.

6. Notes, voices and polyphony

A voice is the group of sound-generating and shaping components used to produce a note. A monophonic synthesiser generally plays one note at a time. A polyphonic synthesiser can allocate multiple voices so several notes sound simultaneously.

A keyboard or MIDI controller usually sends note-on, note-off and velocity information. A synthesiser uses those messages to choose pitches, start envelopes and release notes. MIDI messages are instructions, not recorded audio.

Try thinking like a synthesiser: When you press C–E–G together, a six-voice synthesiser might allocate three voices, each with its own oscillator and envelope. Some designs share filters or other processing between voices.

7. Effects and the output

After the basic sound is generated and shaped, effects can add space, motion and character. Delay repeats a signal; reverb models the persistence and reflections of sound in a space; chorus combines subtly varied signals; and distortion changes waveform shape and typically adds harmonics.

Effects are not exclusive to synthesisers: the same principles apply to guitar pedals, mixing desks and recording software.

8. Putting it all together

A patch is a set of synthesiser settings and connections. Here is a simple recipe for a soft, evolving pad:

Patch recipe: a gentle pad

  1. Start with a sawtooth oscillator, optionally with a slightly detuned second oscillator.
  2. Use a low-pass filter with a moderately low cutoff.
  3. Set a slow attack and a long release on the amplitude envelope.
  4. Use another envelope or a slow LFO to move the filter cutoff gently.
  5. Add a little chorus and reverb.

Now imagine a plucked bass: short attack, fast decay, low sustain, shorter release and a more percussive filter envelope. Many of the same building blocks, but a very different result.

Challenge: Which settings would you change to make a sharp metallic sound? What would make a long, airy sound? Can you explain your choices using frequency, amplitude, harmonics and time?

9. Where next?

We now have the essential vocabulary: oscillators create or supply a signal, filters reshape its spectrum, envelopes shape change over time, and modulation connects one changing signal to another parameter.

Not every synthesiser follows this exact chain. FM changes an oscillator's instantaneous frequency; additive synthesis combines partials; wavetable synthesis reads and moves between stored waveforms; granular synthesis rearranges tiny pieces of recorded sound; and physical modelling simulates aspects of vibrating systems. Those approaches deserve their own deeper lessons.
