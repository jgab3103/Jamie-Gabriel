The Mathematics of Sound

Music Synthesis Primer · Part 1 · Sound, Waves and Numbers

What does a sound actually look like? Why does one note sound higher than another? And how can a synthesiser create a trumpet, a bass or a sound that no acoustic instrument has ever made?

We can begin answering these questions with just a little mathematics. This primer introduces the building blocks of sound synthesis, one idea at a time. You don't need advanced maths: curiosity, a willingness to experiment and a little algebra will do nicely.

1. Sound begins with vibration

Pluck a guitar string, strike a piano key or speak a word: something vibrates. Those vibrations create tiny changes in air pressure that travel outward as sound waves. When they reach our ears, our auditory system interprets them as sound.

Sound needs a medium, such as air, water or a solid, through which to travel. The air particles move back and forth locally; the disturbance travels through the air. A graph of sound usually shows pressure variation over time, not a picture of air moving up and down like a rope.

Important distinction: The familiar wavy line is a graph of a changing measurement. Sound travelling through air is a longitudinal pressure wave.

2. Drawing a wave

A waveform shows how a signal changes with time. The horizontal axis represents time; the vertical axis represents its value (such as pressure variation or an audio signal's amplitude). A smooth, repeating oscillation is called a sine wave.

A sine wave: regular peaks and troughs around a centre line.

One complete repetition is called a cycle. Once we can describe how large a cycle is and how quickly it repeats, we can begin making musical connections.

3. Frequency, period and pitch

Frequency is the number of cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). A 100 Hz wave completes 100 cycles every second. For a simple periodic tone, increasing frequency increases perceived pitch.

The period, written T, is the time taken for one cycle. Frequency and period are reciprocals:

f = 1 / T    and    T = 1 / ff = frequency (Hz); T = period (seconds)

Example: Concert A

The A above middle C is commonly tuned to 440 Hz. Its period is 1 ÷ 440 ≈ 0.00227 seconds, or 2.27 milliseconds.

Double 440 Hz to 880 Hz and the pitch rises one octave. Halve it to 220 Hz and the pitch falls one octave.

In twelve-tone equal temperament, each semitone multiplies frequency by the same factor:

f₂ = f₁ × 2n/12n = number of semitones between the notes

For example, the C one octave above middle C is approximately 523.25 Hz. The relation between frequency and perceived pitch is not perfectly linear, but the octave relationship is especially useful in music.

4. Amplitude and loudness

Amplitude describes the size of a waveform's variation from its centre line. In a simple signal, increasing amplitude increases the signal level. All else being equal, we generally perceive a higher-level sound as louder.

Perceived loudness is more complicated than amplitude alone: our ears respond differently to different frequencies, and loudness also depends on duration and context. Audio levels are often expressed in decibels (dB), which use a logarithmic scale.

Level difference (dB) = 20 log₁₀(A₂ / A₁)For two comparable amplitude measurements, with the same reference

Doubling a signal's amplitude corresponds to approximately +6 dB. This does not necessarily mean that a listener hears it as twice as loud.

5. The sine wave equation

Here is the heart of our mathematical model. A sine wave can be described with one short equation:

x(t) = A sin(2πft + φ)A = amplitude · f = frequency · t = time · φ = phase offset (radians)

Don't worry if the symbols look unfamiliar. The sine function produces a smoothly oscillating value between −1 and +1. Multiplying by A changes its size; f controls how fast it oscillates; and φ moves the wave along its cycle.

6. Phase and combining waves

Two waves can have the same frequency and amplitude but begin at different points in their cycles. This difference is called phase. We can measure it in degrees: 360° is a complete cycle, and 180° is half a cycle.

When signals combine, their instantaneous values add. If two identical sine waves line up, they reinforce one another. If one is exactly 180° out of phase, they cancel in an ideal mathematical sum.

Two waves (solid and dashed) and their sum (dark line). Try changing the phase difference.

These ideas help explain interference, comb filtering and why combining microphones or audio signals sometimes changes their tone dramatically.

7. Harmonics and timbre

Real musical tones are often more complex than a single sine wave. Many can be modelled as a sum of sine waves with different frequencies, amplitudes and phases. When their frequencies are whole-number multiples of a fundamental frequency, we call them harmonics.

For a fundamental of 100 Hz, the first four harmonics are 100, 200, 300 and 400 Hz. Their relative strengths help shape the sound's timbre — the quality that makes a flute different from a violin even when both play the same note.

x(t) = Σ Aₙ sin(2πn f₀ t + φₙ)n = harmonic number; f₀ = fundamental frequency; Aₙ and φₙ describe each harmonic

The Σ symbol simply means add them together. This is a basic idea behind Fourier analysis, which helps us understand complicated sounds by studying their frequency components.

Not all sounds are perfectly harmonic or periodic. Bells, cymbals, noisy textures and many percussion sounds contain inharmonic or broadband components. That's part of what makes sound design so interesting.

8. Turning sound into numbers

A computer cannot store a continuous analogue waveform directly. Instead, digital audio represents a signal using a sequence of measurements called samples, taken at regular time intervals.

The sample rate tells us how many samples are taken per second. At 44,100 Hz (44.1 kHz), we take 44,100 samples per second, for each channel.

tₙ = n / fₛtₙ = time of sample n; fₛ = sample rate

The Nyquist frequency is half the sample rate. For a 44.1 kHz signal, it is 22.05 kHz. To avoid aliasing, frequencies at or above this boundary must be appropriately filtered before sampling or when generating digital signals.

Bit depth determines how many quantisation levels are available for representing sample amplitude in standard PCM audio. For example, 16-bit PCM has 2¹⁶ = 65,536 possible levels. Sample rate and bit depth describe different properties: how frequently we measure, and how finely each measurement is represented.

A continuous waveform (line) and discrete samples (dots), illustrated at a deliberately low sample density.

Once audio is represented as numbers, a synthesiser can generate, combine, transform and filter those numbers to create new sounds.

9. Try it yourself

Three little experiments

  1. Octaves: Set the frequency to 220 Hz, then 440 Hz, then 880 Hz. What changes on the graph, and what changes in your hearing?
  2. Phase: Set the phase difference to 0°, then 180°. What happens to the sum of the two waves?
  3. Timbre: Add more second and third harmonic content. How does the waveform's shape change even though its fundamental frequency stays at 100 Hz?

That's the mathematical foundation. Next we can explore how synthesis techniques use these ideas to build sounds: oscillators, filters, envelopes, modulation and beyond.
