John Chowning and the Birth of FM Synthesis

A guided study of The Synthesis of Complex Audio Spectra by Means of Frequency Modulation (1973) · Music synthesis and acoustics

In 1973, composer and computer-music pioneer John Chowning published one of the defining papers in digital sound synthesis. His insight was astonishingly economical: two simple oscillators could generate a whole family of complex spectra, and a handful of parameters could make those spectra evolve musically through time.

The paper, published in the Journal of the Audio Engineering Society, helped establish the foundations of digital FM synthesis, later popularised by instruments such as the Yamaha DX7. But it is much more than a historical account of an electronic keyboard. It is a demonstration of the relationship between mathematical structure, auditory perception and the design of musical instruments.

1. The central principle: frequency creates timbre

A single sine-wave oscillator produces one frequency and no harmonic complexity. Its output can be written:

\[x(t)=A\sin(2\pi f_ct)\]

Here \(A\) is amplitude, \(f_c\) is the carrier frequency and \(t\) is time. Chowning's method changes the instantaneous frequency of this carrier using another sine-wave oscillator: the modulator.

\[\boxed{x(t)=A\sin\left(2\pi f_ct+I\sin(2\pi f_mt)\right)}\]

The new terms are \(f_m\), the modulator frequency, and \(I\), the modulation index. The nested sine function is the key: the two oscillators are not simply being mixed together. The modulator changes the carrier's phase and therefore its instantaneous frequency.

The result contains sidebands, with frequencies at:

\[\boxed{f=f_c\pm nf_m,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots}\]

For example, with a carrier of 440 Hz and a modulator of 110 Hz, potential components appear at 440 Hz, 330 and 550 Hz, 220 and 660 Hz, 110 and 770 Hz, and so on. One modulated oscillator generates an entire spectrum.

Schematic waveform comparison. In FM, the wave cycles compress and expand as the instantaneous frequency changes.

2. Modulation index: controlling spectral complexity

The modulation index is defined by the ratio of peak frequency deviation to modulator frequency:

\[\boxed{I=\frac{\Delta f}{f_m}}\]

If the modulator frequency is 100 Hz and the peak frequency deviation is 500 Hz, then \(I=5\). Increasing the index generally spreads energy across more sidebands, making the sound spectrally richer. Importantly, the original carrier need not remain the strongest component.

Unlike a subtractive synthesiser, which filters an initially rich waveform, FM changes the distribution of energy among the partials as part of the generation process itself.

3. Why Bessel functions appear

The mathematical elegance of FM comes from expanding the nested sine function into sinusoidal components using Bessel functions of the first kind:

\[x(t)=A\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(I)\sin\left((\omega_c+n\omega_m)t\right)\]

Here \(J_n(I)\) determines the signed amplitude of the sideband of order \(n\). The carrier is governed by \(J_0(I)\), the first sidebands by \(J_1(I)\), the second by \(J_2(I)\), and so on.

ComponentCoefficient at \(I=1\)
Carrier \(J_0(1)\)0.765
First sideband \(J_1(1)\)0.440
Second sideband \(J_2(1)\)0.115
Third sideband \(J_3(1)\)0.020
Fourth sideband \(J_4(1)\)0.002

The coefficients can be negative, representing phase inversion rather than negative acoustic energy. This matters because sidebands that meet at the same frequency can reinforce or cancel. The resulting changes in amplitude are nonlinear: a component can disappear and reappear as the index increases.

Important distinction. Increasing the index generally broadens the spectrum, but it does not monotonically increase every harmonic. Individual Bessel coefficients oscillate with the index.

4. Harmonic and inharmonic spectra

The ratio between carrier and modulator frequencies determines the spacing and structure of the sidebands:

\[R=\frac{f_c}{f_m}\]

Harmonic example: 400 Hz and 100 Hz

A 4:1 ratio generates components at 400 ± 100 Hz, 400 ± 200 Hz, and so forth. These occupy integer multiples of 100 Hz, although their relative strengths depend on the modulation index. The perceived pitch may therefore correspond to a missing or weak fundamental rather than to the carrier itself.

Inharmonic example: 400 Hz and 283 Hz

A ratio near \(\sqrt{2}\) produces sidebands such as 117, 683, 166, 966, 449 and 1249 Hz. The negative-frequency terms in the algebra are reflected into the positive-frequency spectrum with their phase signs accounted for. These components do not resemble the low-order harmonic series of a conventional pitched instrument.

Strictly, the ratio 400/283 is rational and therefore has a mathematical common fundamental of 1 Hz. The exact irrational ratio \(\sqrt{2}\) is truly inharmonic. In practical musical terms, both arrangements can create a metallic, ambiguous pitch impression.

Two complementary controls: the frequency ratio sets the architecture of the spectrum; the modulation index governs how energy is distributed within it.

Special rational ratios can also emphasise selected harmonic families. For example, the 3:2 ratio used in Chowning's clarinet-like example places its components on odd-numbered harmonics of a common fundamental.

5. Timbre is a process, not a static spectrum

Perhaps Chowning's deepest musical insight is that the evolution of a sound's spectrum matters as much as its steady-state content. Acoustic instruments change their spectral balance during the attack, sustain and release of a note.

We can make both the amplitude and modulation index time-dependent:

\[x(t)=A(t)\sin\left(2\pi f_ct+I(t)\sin(2\pi f_mt)\right)\]

Here \(A(t)\) controls the loudness envelope and \(I(t)\) controls spectral complexity. Two notes with the same amplitude envelope can sound radically different if their index envelopes differ.

━━ Amplitude envelope   ┄┄ Modulation-index envelope. Illustrative curves: spectral richness decreases faster than loudness.

In subtractive synthesis, a low-pass filter might make a note progressively darker. In FM, the index envelope changes the sideband distribution directly. Both can create a similar broad perceptual trajectory, but their spectral details differ.

6. Four families of instrument examples

Brass: brightness follows intensity

Brass instruments typically become spectrally brighter as they are played more forcefully. An FM approximation can couple the modulation index to the amplitude envelope, for example \(I(t)=5E(t)\), where \(E(t)\) varies between 0 and 1. As the note grows louder, the spectrum expands. This is an illustrative experiment, not a transcription of a particular historical preset.

Woodwinds: harmonic selection

Chowning discusses bassoon-like and clarinet-like sounds. One bassoon-like configuration uses a 500 Hz carrier and a 100 Hz modulator (5:1). A clarinet-like configuration uses 900 Hz and 600 Hz (3:2), creating an odd-harmonic family relative to 300 Hz. The paper also explores relationships in which the modulation index varies inversely with the amplitude envelope.

Bells: a complex spectrum that decays

A bell-like example uses a 200 Hz carrier and a 280 Hz modulator, with a high initial modulation index of approximately 10 and a long exponential decay. The 5:7 ratio produces a metallic spectral pattern. One useful simplified model is:

\[A(t)=A_0e^{-t/\tau_A},\qquad I(t)=I_0e^{-t/\tau_I}\]

When the index decays more quickly than the amplitude, the sound loses its metallic complexity while the residual tone continues to ring. A real bell has partials with partly independent decay rates, so a simple two-operator patch is an approximation rather than a physical model.

Wooden percussion: rapid spectral evolution

A wood-drum-like example uses a carrier of 80 Hz, a modulator of 55 Hz and a modulation index that can reach 25. A high index creates a burst of complex spectral activity at the attack; rapid envelope changes give the impression of a struck, resonant object.

7. Implementation, efficiency and the DX7

Chowning developed these ideas at Stanford in an era when computer processing was scarce. Additive synthesis might require dozens of separately controlled oscillators to construct a complex spectrum. FM could produce rich, evolving spectra with only a few oscillators and arithmetic operations.

The Yamaha DX7, introduced in 1983, extended this principle into a six-operator instrument. Operators could be connected in different algorithms, allowing several carrier/modulator relationships and envelopes to interact. Modern FM synthesisers continue this tradition, often combining FM with filtering, waveshaping and other synthesis methods.

Chowning did not invent frequency modulation as a physical or mathematical process: FM was already well known in communications engineering. His breakthrough was to recognise its extraordinary potential for audio-frequency sound synthesis.

9. Why the paper remains seminal

Chowning's paper demonstrates four interrelated principles: spectral economy, because a few oscillators can create complex spectra; mathematical control, because ratios and indices organise those spectra; dynamic timbre, because index envelopes transform sounds through time; and musical generality, because the same architecture can suggest brass, woodwinds, bells, percussion and sounds with no acoustic counterpart.

There is a deeper distinction here between reconstructing a sound's spectrum and constructing a convincing generative process. Additive synthesis can represent individual partials with great precision. FM sacrifices some independent control over those partials in exchange for a compact, coherent and musically expressive system.

In this sense, Chowning's paper is as much about auditory perception as it is about oscillator mathematics. Timbre is not merely a static list of frequencies. It is something that unfolds through time.

The central lesson: FM is not simply a way to make metallic sounds. It is a way to organise the evolution of spectral energy with a small number of meaningful controls.
