Rational Trigonometry and Sound Synthesis

A mathematical and philosophical primer · Inspired by Norman Wildberger

What if we reconsidered the mathematics of sound synthesis from the ground up? What if continuous waves, irrational numbers and infinite series were not our starting point?

This primer explores the mathematics of Australian mathematician Norman J. Wildberger and asks what his approach might mean for electronic music, digital oscillators, tuning and the representation of sound.

It is partly a mathematical introduction, partly a philosophical thought experiment. Wildberger developed rational trigonometry as a framework for geometry, not as a theory of sound synthesis. The connections explored here are extensions of his ideas, not claims he has made about synthesiser design.

1. Why question the mathematics of synthesis?

Almost every modern synthesiser rests, directly or indirectly, on a mathematical model of oscillation. The familiar sine wave is defined by:

\[x(t)=A\sin(2\pi ft+\phi)\]

This elegant equation contains several powerful assumptions: time is treated as continuous, the waveform can be evaluated at arbitrary real-valued instants, and sine is defined through a mathematical framework involving limits and, commonly, infinite processes.

Yet a digital synthesiser does not generate an infinitely continuous mathematical function. It calculates a finite sequence of numerical values. At a sampling rate of 48,000 Hz, one second of mono audio contains exactly 48,000 samples.

Food for thought: Why do we begin with an infinite mathematical idealisation when the instrument itself performs finite arithmetic?

Wildberger's broader mathematical work challenges the necessity of relying on the real-number continuum and completed infinities as foundational objects. Rather than beginning with continuous quantities, he favours algebraic relationships that can be described exactly through rational operations.

This does not mean conventional synthesis mathematics is invalid. It raises a different question: might there be another, more explicitly computational way to formulate some of the same musical processes?

2. Wildberger's alternative: rational trigonometry

Wildberger introduced rational trigonometry systematically in his 2005 book Divine Proportions: Rational Trigonometry to Universal Geometry. His central proposal is to replace two familiar geometric quantities: distance becomes quadrance, and angle becomes spread.

The resulting geometry can express many familiar theorems through addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, without requiring square roots or conventional trigonometric functions. This is especially appealing for computational applications because rational inputs often produce exact rational outputs.

Quadrance: distance without square roots

Classical Euclidean distance between two points is:

\[d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\]

Rational trigonometry instead uses quadrance:

\[\boxed{Q=(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\]

For the diagonal of a unit square, classical distance is \(\sqrt{2}\), whereas quadrance is exactly \(2\). From Wildberger's perspective, the square root is not necessarily a fundamental geometric requirement. It is an additional operation introduced by our choice to work with distance rather than squared distance.

The synthesis connection: Digital signal processing frequently uses squared quantities already: signal power, mean-square amplitude, energy and squared error. Root-mean-square amplitude introduces a square root, but many comparisons and calculations can be performed directly with mean-square values. This does not make RMS obsolete; it shows how changing the fundamental quantity can simplify certain computations.

3. Spread: an alternative to angles

Angles are central to synthesis. We describe oscillators in terms of phase angles, often using radians. We use sine and cosine for oscillation, Fourier analysis and phase relationships.

Wildberger replaces the angle between lines with a quantity called spread. For an angle \(\theta\), the corresponding spread is:

\[\boxed{s=\sin^2(\theta)}\]

This is the connection to classical trigonometry, not the fundamental algebraic definition of spread.

Classical angleSpread
0°0
30°1/4
45°1/2
60°3/4
90°1

Spread is always between 0 and 1 in Euclidean geometry. It measures the separation of unoriented lines, so 30° and 150° have the same spread. More importantly, spread can be calculated without computing an angle.

Given direction vectors \(\mathbf{u}=(a,b)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(c,d)\):

\[\boxed{s=\frac{(ad-bc)^2}{(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)}}\]

For \(\mathbf{u}=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(1,1)\), we obtain \(s=1/2\). Everything is exact and rational.

What does this mean for synthesis?

A conventional oscillator describes phase as a directed angle moving around a circle. Could we describe phase using spread instead?

Not directly. Spread loses information about orientation and direction: several different phase angles can have the same spread. A musical oscillator must distinguish those phases if it is to produce a complete waveform.

This is an important limitation, but also a useful starting point. It suggests that a rational approach to synthesis would need an algebraic representation of directed phase, rather than simply replacing phase angles with spreads.

4. Pythagoras, the spread law and the cross law

Wildberger's framework preserves much of classical Euclidean geometry, but rewrites its relationships. The Pythagorean theorem becomes particularly simple:

\[\boxed{Q_1+Q_2=Q_3}\]

There is no need to square the lengths, because quadrances are already squared distances. The classical sine rule becomes the spread law:

\[\boxed{\frac{s_1}{Q_1}=\frac{s_2}{Q_2}=\frac{s_3}{Q_3}}\]

Each spread is opposite the side with the corresponding quadrance. The cosine rule has an algebraic counterpart called the cross law:

\[\boxed{(Q_1+Q_2-Q_3)^2=4Q_1Q_2(1-s_3)}\]

The triple spread formula relates three spreads associated with three lines:

\[\boxed{(s_1+s_2+s_3)^2=2(s_1^2+s_2^2+s_3^2)+4s_1s_2s_3}\]

These relationships can be expressed without explicit trigonometric functions. This is one of Wildberger's most significant achievements: a large body of geometric reasoning can be reconstructed algebraically, often with exact rational calculations.

For synthesis, the philosophical lesson is that the familiar mathematical representation is not necessarily the only possible representation.

5. The circle: can we generate oscillation without sine?

The classical unit circle is described by \(x^2+y^2=1\). Usually, we parameterise it using \(x=\cos(\theta)\) and \(y=\sin(\theta)\). But there is another representation:

\[\boxed{x=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad y=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}}\]

For rational \(t\), both coordinates are rational.

For example, at \(t=1/2\), \(x=3/5\) and \(y=4/5\). This produces the familiar 3–4–5 relationship.

The connection to synthesis is tempting: could we use the rational parameter \(t\) to generate a periodic waveform? Yes, but there is a complication. Moving \(t\) at a constant rate does not produce uniform angular motion around the circle. The resulting waveform would not be a conventional sinusoid, and the parametrisation omits one point of the circle for finite \(t\).

That could be considered a problem if our goal is an exact sine oscillator. But if our goal is sound design, a nonuniform traversal may produce an interesting waveform with additional harmonic content. The mathematics becomes not merely a way of reproducing existing oscillators, but a potential source of new synthesis methods.

6. Fourier synthesis and the problem of infinity

Fourier analysis underpins much of electronic music. A periodic signal can be represented through a series of harmonic components:

\[x(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(a_k\cos(k\omega_0t)+b_k\sin(k\omega_0t)\right)\]

In practice, an additive synthesiser uses a finite number of oscillators.

Wildberger's philosophical objection to completed infinity invites us to reconsider the status of the infinite Fourier series. Is the infinite series an actual mathematical object, or a convenient idealisation of finite harmonic processes?

A finite Fourier transform or a finite additive synthesis model does not require infinitely many oscillators. Yet infinite-series theory is extremely useful for establishing convergence, error bounds and approximation properties.

The important distinction is between the theoretical model and its finite implementation. A rational or finitist approach could begin with finite harmonic collections as fundamental objects, rather than treating them as approximations to an ideal infinite expansion.

7. Tuning, irrational numbers and musical intervals

Equal temperament offers another philosophical connection. In twelve-tone equal temperament, the frequency ratio of a semitone is:

\[r=2^{1/12}\]

This is irrational. After twelve semitones, the frequency doubles: \(r^{12}=2\). By contrast, just intonation uses rational frequency ratios.

IntervalEqual temperamentJust intonation
Perfect fifth\(2^{7/12}\)\(3/2\)
Major third\(2^{4/12}\)\(5/4\)
Perfect fourth\(2^{5/12}\)\(4/3\)
Octave\(2\)\(2/1\)

Rational tuning has a particularly direct relationship with periodic waveforms. Oscillators at 200 Hz and 300 Hz have a frequency ratio of 3:2. Their cycles align periodically, and the combined waveform repeats every 10 milliseconds.

An irrational frequency ratio does not produce an exactly repeating combined waveform in the ideal continuous mathematical model. This distinction is interesting for synthesis because rational relationships can produce exact common periods, while irrational relationships can produce quasiperiodic behaviour.

However, practical digital oscillators use finite numerical representations. Even an irrational frequency ratio must be approximated when implemented computationally.

Food for thought: What is gained by defining tuning through an ideal irrational ratio when the instrument ultimately works with finite numerical approximations? The answer may depend on whether our priority is mathematical elegance, musical behaviour, computational efficiency or perceptual accuracy.

8. A philosophical question: is the sine wave real?

We often describe the sine wave as the simplest possible sound. It has a single frequency, no harmonic overtones and a mathematically elegant form.

But an exact sine wave extending indefinitely in both directions of time is an idealisation. No physical oscillator can generate such a waveform for all time. Real systems have finite durations, noise, nonlinearities and measurement limitations. Likewise, a digital sine oscillator generates a sequence of finite-precision values.

This raises three distinct questions:

  • Mathematical: Does a perfect continuous sine wave exist as a mathematical object?
  • Physical: Can a perfect continuous sine wave exist in the natural world?
  • Computational: Does a synthesiser need a perfect continuous sine wave to produce the sound we recognise as sinusoidal?

Wildberger's philosophy is most directly concerned with the first question. His criticism of conventional foundations does not, by itself, establish an answer to the second. The third is much easier to answer: no. Finite numerical methods can generate excellent approximations to sinusoidal sound.

This distinction matters because we should not confuse a critique of mathematical foundations with evidence about the physical nature of sound.

9. What might a rational synthesiser look like?

Imagine designing a synthesiser around the principle that every internal operation should be finite and algebraically explicit.

ComponentPossible rational approach
OscillatorsInteger phase accumulators and rational waveform parametrisations
TuningRational frequency ratios
FiltersRational-coefficient difference equations
ModulationFinite algebraic recurrence relations
Spectral analysisFinite discrete transforms
AmplitudeFixed-point or rational arithmetic
Phase relationshipsAlgebraic coordinates and directed phase representations

There is an important caveat: these methods are not necessarily new, and many are already common in digital signal processing. The distinctive contribution would be the decision to treat finite algebraic computation as the foundational mathematical model, rather than merely an approximation to continuous mathematics.

Whether that would lead to better synthesis algorithms remains an open engineering question.

10. The deeper philosophical issue: models versus reality

Perhaps the most interesting contribution of Wildberger's thinking is epistemological. Mathematics gives us models of the world. But a model is not the same thing as the phenomenon it describes.

Mathematical model

Continuous functions, irrational numbers, infinite series

↓
Computational process

Finite samples, numerical algorithms, discrete state

↓
Physical sound

Electrical signals, vibrating loudspeakers, acoustic pressure

Conventional mathematical analysis often moves smoothly between these levels because the models are extraordinarily effective. Wildberger challenges us to examine whether some of the mathematical assumptions are necessary or merely convenient.

This connects with broader philosophical questions about mathematical realism and formalism. A mathematical realist might regard real numbers and continuous functions as legitimate abstract objects, whether or not they can be represented exactly in a computer. A finitist or constructivist may place greater emphasis on objects and operations that can be explicitly constructed or computed.

Neither position automatically determines how physical sound behaves. But each can influence which questions we ask and which mathematical tools we develop.

11. Food for thought: could rational mathematics produce new sounds?

There are several intriguing directions for experimentation.

  • Rational oscillators. Rather than using a sine lookup table or polynomial approximation, explore rational parametrisations and algebraic recurrences to generate periodic waveforms.
  • Rational modulation. Investigate whether finite rational relationships between oscillators produce useful families of periodic or evolving timbres.
  • Algebraic waveshaping. Construct nonlinear transfer functions from ratios of polynomials, then examine their harmonic spectra and numerical behaviour.
  • Exact tuning structures. Explore networks of oscillators with rational frequency relationships, especially where complex periodicity and beating patterns emerge.
  • Finite spectral synthesis. Develop synthesis models in which the finite harmonic structure is primary, with no conceptual dependence on an infinite series.

None of these requires rejecting conventional DSP. In fact, comparing the approaches using established signal-processing methods would be essential.

12. Closing reflection

Wildberger's rational trigonometry demonstrates that familiar mathematical structures can sometimes be reformulated using surprisingly different foundations. Quadrance replaces distance. Spread replaces angle. Geometric theorems become rational algebraic identities.

The relevance to synthesis is not that conventional trigonometry should be abandoned. Rather, it invites us to question whether the mathematical language we inherited is always the most natural language for the computational systems we build.

A digital synthesiser is, fundamentally, a finite machine producing finite numerical representations of sound. Yet much of its theoretical vocabulary comes from the mathematics of continuity and infinity.

There is no contradiction in this: continuous mathematics provides exceptionally powerful tools for understanding discrete systems. But there is an interesting intellectual opportunity in reversing the perspective.

What happens if we begin with finite computation, exact algebraic relationships and discrete musical structures, and treat continuity as an emergent approximation rather than a foundational assumption?

That question sits at the intersection of mathematics, computer science, philosophy and musical creativity. And it may offer a productive way of thinking differently about sound synthesis.
