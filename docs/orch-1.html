What You Need to Know Before Learning Composition Through Score Reading

16 August 2026

One of the most useful ways to learn composition is by studying scores. You can learn a huge amount by looking closely at what other composers have written: how they organise musical ideas, distribute material between instruments, create movement, and solve compositional problems.

But before this becomes useful, there is one fairly basic skill you need: you should be able to look at a score and know what notes you are looking at without having to stop and work them out.

This sounds obvious, but orchestral scores can be considerably more complicated to read than piano music. Different instruments use different clefs, and many instruments do not sound the pitch that is written on the page.

1. You need to know the clefs

You should be able to look at a note in a score and identify its pitch immediately, regardless of which common clef is being used.

At a minimum, this means being comfortable with:

  • Treble clef
  • Bass clef
  • Alto clef
  • Tenor clef

Treble and bass clefs will account for a large proportion of what you encounter, but alto and tenor clefs are common enough in orchestral and chamber music that they are worth learning properly.

The important distinction is between knowing how the clef works and actually being able to read it. If you have to count up from a reference note every time you encounter tenor clef, you haven't quite reached the level of fluency needed for effortless score reading.

2. You need to understand transposing instruments

The second essential skill is understanding that the note written in a part is not always the note that you hear.

2. You need to understand transposing instruments

The second essential skill is understanding that the note written in a part is not always the note that actually sounds. This is because some instruments are transposing instruments.

The important thing is to be able to think about this from both sides: what the player sees and plays, and what you hear when that note is played.

Instrument When the player reads C What the score reader needs to know What the composer needs to do
B♭ Trumpet Sounds B♭ Read the written note one whole tone lower as concert pitch. Write the part one whole tone higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.
B♭ Clarinet Sounds B♭ Read the written note one whole tone lower as concert pitch. Write the part one whole tone higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.
F Horn Sounds F Read the written note a perfect fifth lower as concert pitch. Write the part a perfect fifth higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.
E♭ Alto Saxophone Sounds E♭ Read the written note a major sixth lower as concert pitch. Write the part a major sixth higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.
B♭ Tenor Saxophone Sounds B♭, one octave lower Read the written note a major ninth lower as concert pitch. Write the part a major ninth higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.
E♭ Baritone Saxophone Sounds E♭, one octave lower Read the written note a major thirteenth lower as concert pitch. Write the part a major thirteenth higher than the desired concert pitch, and use the appropriate transposed key signature.

For example, if you see a written C in a B♭ trumpet part, the trumpet player plays the written C. But when reading the full score, you need to understand that the resulting concert pitch is B♭.

You don't necessarily need to memorise every possible transposition immediately. The goal is to become comfortable enough with the common instruments that you can look at a written part, recognise its transposition, and understand the sounding pitch without having to stop and puzzle it out.

The basic goal

So, before beginning serious composition study through score reading, aim for a very simple level of fluency:

  • You can identify any note in treble, bass, alto and tenor clefs.
  • You can read notes without having to count lines and spaces each time.
  • You understand which common instruments are transposing instruments.
  • You can work out the sounding pitch of a written note on a transposing instrument.

Once these things become automatic, a score stops looking like a collection of symbols that need to be decoded. You can simply look at it and read the music.

That is the point at which score reading can really start to become a tool for learning composition.
